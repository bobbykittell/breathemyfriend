Step 1 · Body

Settle the body

You can't think your way out of a body that's in alarm. So we start with the body.

In one line

Your nervous system listens to your body more than your thoughts. Loosen it, shake it, breathe it slower, and the alarm turns down.

The usual approach

"Just think positive." "Calm down." Trying to talk a panicking body out of panicking.

This approach

Give the body a new experience first. Once it feels safer, the mind follows on its own.

Your week

Three practices. Small, steady doses beat big, rare ones.

Stretch the web

Under your skin is fascia: a thin, stretchy web that wraps every muscle, bone and organ. Think of the white film on a chicken breast. It's all one connected sheet, head to toe.

Why it matters

Fascia is packed with sensing nerves. It's one of the ways your brain "reads" how safe your body is. When it's tight and stuck, the brain reads tension, and tension feels like danger.

What stress does

Stress makes you brace: shoulders up, jaw tight, belly held. Do that for years and fascia stiffens around the brace. The body stays braced even when life is calm.

What stretching does

Slow, whole-body movement unsticks the web. Many people feel warmth, tingling, or a wave of release. Energy that felt stuck starts to move again.

How to do it
  1. Follow a short daily routine from Human Garage. A few minutes is enough to start.
  2. Move slowly. Fascia responds to gentle, sustained stretch, not force.
  3. Notice what you feel during and after. That noticing is half the practice.

Shake it off

Watch a dog after a scare or a fight. It shakes its whole body, then trots off like nothing happened. Many mammals do this. It looks like a reset button for stress.

Humans have the same ability, but we learned to hold still and "keep it together." TRE (Tension and Trauma Releasing Exercises, created by David Berceli) helps you switch that natural shaking back on.

What it feels like

A gentle, automatic trembling, usually starting in the legs. You're not making it happen. You're letting it happen.

Why it helps

The shaking seems to burn off leftover stress charge, the energy your body got ready to fight or run with but never used.

How long

About 10 to 15 minutes of shaking, twice a week. More is not better here.

How to do it
  1. Do a few simple exercises that tire the legs a little (a TRE guide or provider will show you the set).
  2. Lie on your back, soles of the feet together, knees falling open. Then slowly raise the knees a few inches.
  3. Let the trembling start on its own. Breathe and watch it.
  4. To stop: stretch your legs out flat. The shaking settles. Learn this first, so you always feel in charge.

Go gently

  • Shaking can bring up old feelings or memories. If it feels like too much, stop, sit up and rest.
  • Skip it during pregnancy, with a fresh injury, or with serious mental-health conditions unless a certified TRE provider is guiding you.

Breathe less, not more

The myth

CO₂ is waste. Breathe big and deep to get it out and get more oxygen in.

The truth

You need CO₂. It's what helps your blood hand oxygen to your cells. Breathe too much and you can end up feeling short of air.

Anxious people often overbreathe without knowing it: fast, upper-chest, through the mouth, lots of sighing. That drops CO₂, and low CO₂ causes dizziness, tingling hands, a racing heart and air hunger. Those are panic symptoms. The breathing can be feeding the panic.

The Buteyko Method (from Ukrainian doctor Konstantin Buteyko) trains you to breathe lighter, slower and through the nose, so your body gets comfortable with a healthy level of CO₂.

Your progress number: the Control Pause
  1. Sit calmly. Breathe normally through your nose for a minute.
  2. After a normal, relaxed breath out, pinch your nose and start counting seconds.
  3. Stop at the first urge to breathe. Not the max. Just the first nudge.
  4. Let go and breathe in calmly through the nose. If you need to gasp, you held too long.

Check it once a day, at the same time. Watching the number climb over weeks is a great motivator.

The daily practice
  1. Keep your mouth closed. Nose breathing, day and night as much as you can.
  2. Sit and breathe a little less than you want to: smaller, softer, slower breaths.
  3. Aim for a light, mild air hunger, like you'd enjoy a slightly bigger breath. Hold that gently for a few minutes.

Go gently, especially with panic

  • People with panic are often extra sensitive to rising CO₂. A long breath hold can trigger the very feeling you're trying to calm.
  • Start with very short, very easy reductions. Comfort first. Never strain.
  • Check with a doctor first if you're pregnant or have heart problems, high blood pressure, epilepsy or kidney disease.
